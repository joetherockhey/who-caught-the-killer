Who caught the killer?

The Mentalist — Season 1, Episode 1

Patrick Jane is a consultant with no badge and no gun. The CBI detectives do the records, the forensics and the arrests. So who actually catches the killer? I scored all 23 episodes. This is the first.

What changed

Take 1 was factually wrong in five places and spent its first 16 seconds explaining itself before anything happened. Take 2 opens on a man being shot.

Take 1 opened with“The Mentalist is one of the most popular detective shows ever made.” Then three more lines of setup. The first actual event arrives at 18.7 seconds.

The look

A separate research pass on the Vox / Johnny Harris style. The finding that mattered: the style is deliberate imperfection. Nothing sits square, edges are torn rather than cut, and the graphics are held on a 12 fps grid while the camera moves at 24 — a smooth push under a stuttering overlay.

The important part: no new images were generated. The newspaper-clipping look is a compositing problem, not a generation problem. The same 17 stills are re-staged as physical photographs on an investigation board — torn cream edges, 85-line newsprint halftone, cellophane tape, a pushpin, scrap paper behind.

Built in the rendererTorn deckle edges, halftone dots, tape and pins, hand-drawn chinagraph circles that overshoot their own closure, highlighter swipes, under-inked rubber stamps, per-shot camera moves.
CostImages: nothing — all compositing. Voice: about 6 credits for 20 lines through ElevenLabs. The whole rebuild ran on the free tier.

Known problems

  • The cold open has no purpose-built image. The first three lines are the entire hook — a shooting, an accusation, a wife with a gun — and all three are played by stills made for other beats. This is the biggest single weakness.
  • Nothing in the image set shows a woman. Both women in this script — the one who fires the gun and the one killed as cover — are invisible.
  • No image shows violence. The set is all aftermath. A video built on two murders never shows a shot fired.
  • One line runs against the episode’s chronology. The diner scene actually happens before the lab result. It’s narrated as a parallel track rather than a sequence, which is true but asks something of the viewer.
  • The verdict line is the contestable one. The detectives’ 20% includes real work — the interrogation that produced the affair, and the arrest itself, which Jane could not have made. The claim is that their key evidence was planted, not all of it.

What I'd do next

  • Three bespoke images, three credits. The shooting, the wife fetching the gun, and a gloved hand laying the planted hair. The prompts are already written.
  • Sound design on the cuts. Every hard cut should land on an audio hit. Right now the cut carries a small camera shake and nothing else.
  • Test the hook. Two versions of the first line, everything else identical.

How the split is scored

Three weighted components, applied the same way to every episode: identification (50%) — who worked out who did it; evidence and legwork (30%) — forensics, records, canvassing, surveillance; resolution (20%) — who produced the confession or the arrest.

The forensic evidence was real, and it was aimed at innocent men — because the killer planted it. Jane identified him, manufactured the evidence and sprang the trap. The CBI contribution was the file review, the interrogation and the arrest — necessary, but not what broke the case.
