Who caught the killer?

The Mentalist — Season 1

Patrick Jane is a consultant with no badge and no gun. The CBI detectives do the records, the forensics and the arrests. So who actually catches the killer? I am scoring all 23 episodes, in order. Every take of every episode is kept here.

It finally says what it is

Take 3 said “I'm scoring every episode” and never said scoring what. The bar was labelled on screen, but a viewer listening rather than reading got nothing — and “scoring” without a metric sounds like a review score out of ten.

The opening now states the premise in the channel owner's own words: a rewatch, Jane as a reformed fake psychic turned California Bureau of Investigation consultant, the observation that he seems to do far more of the legwork than the detectives, and the question the whole series asks — how much of the credit for catching the killer belongs to Jane, and how much to them.

It costs 27 seconds before the shooting, against 10 in Take 3. That is the trade, made deliberately: the video is now comprehensible to someone who has never seen the show, which it demonstrably was not before.

Four images that didn't exist

The biggest weakness in every previous take was that the stills were made for an older script, so the pictures and the words often described different scenes. Four purpose-made images fix the worst of it.

The shootingTake 3 opened on “a man is shot dead” over an empty interrogation room. It now shows the shooting — the cream suit, the collapse, the teacup breaking mid-air, the muzzle flash.
The wifeNo previous version had a woman on screen anywhere, in a story where one fires the gun and another is murdered as cover.
The planted hairThe turn of the whole video was a repeated frame with a stamp on it. It is now a gloved hand and a pair of tweezers.
The motiveThe ledger, the cash, the photograph face down — replacing a closed manila folder.
One word“…and how much to them” became “…and how much to the detectives”. “Them” had no antecedent that early, and the bar's own label says THE DETECTIVES.

Wide images are mounted whole rather than cropped. The first attempt cropped the shooting to a square and lost Jane on one edge and the muzzle flash on the other — the two things that made it the shooting.

Every line went back to the writer

The channel owner caught a broken line — “a woman and her doctor, staged as Red John”, where the grammar attaches the staging to the victims rather than the scene — and made the sharper point that he should not be the one catching it. He was right. The line had survived four takes because it was being hand-patched rather than sent back to the agent whose job it was.

So the whole script went back for a line-by-line audit, read aloud as a stranger hears it: once, at speed, no rewind. Nineteen of the twenty-one lines had a real defect. Most were the same family — a pronoun resolving to the wrong man.

WasWhat a listener heardNow

Two fixes went deeper than grammar. “Uncaught.” as a one-word sentence landed as a dropped word — and was doing the wrong job anyway, because “wrong wall” three lines later had never been set up. It now says where Red John paints his smiley, which turns the next line from atmosphere into a deduction. And “years earlier” was added to the Red John line, because two murdered daughters were landing four lines apart and a listener merged them into one.

The opening, in the channel owner's words

The premise is now fixed across the series and written out in full, rather than compressed by whoever drafted it:

“On a recent rewatch of The Mentalist, I noticed something I hadn't previously seen. Jane, a reformed grifting psychic turned CBI consultant, works with a team of detectives to solve murders. What is strange though is that Jane seems to do far more of the legwork than the detectives. So in this series I plan to analyse each episode, to answer the question… how much credit for catching the killer belongs to Jane, and how much to the detectives.” 27 seconds — the same five lines open every episode

One phrase in there does work none of the earlier drafts managed: “works with a team of detectives”. It establishes that they are his team, not his opponents — so the scoreboard reads as splitting credit rather than picking a fight, which is what the series is actually doing.

The captions now size themselves to the line. The longest line of the opening is 113 characters; at the old fixed size it would have run off the bottom of the frame and under the phone's own controls, where it looks fine in the render and broken on a handset.

It ends on a question

The last words used to be “Episode two” — a sign-off that asked nothing. It now closes on “My analysis: eighty-twenty to Jane for catching this killer. But what do you think?”

Three things that line does. “My analysis” makes the number a judgement rather than a reported fact — a viewer test on an earlier cut read the score as objective and so never thought to disagree with it. “for catching this killer” restates the metric and separates it from Red John, who is named earlier and explicitly is not caught. And the question is an invitation, not a dare: platforms downrank explicit engagement asks, and an open question draws sentences rather than one-word replies.

The bar argues now

It used to glide continuously against the video's progress, which made it read as decoration — or, to one tester, as a viewers' poll. It now holds perfectly still except on the nine lines that actually award credit to someone, and moves only during those.

The sequence makes the case on its own: level through the entire premise, up when Jane names the father, down when the detectives confirm the staging, down again when their lab pulls the hair, sharply back up when that hair turns out to have been planted, up through the trap, then down a notch when a detective makes the arrest Jane could not. Eighty–twenty, arrived at rather than announced.

The voice is now free, forever

Take 3's narration was a paid API at 0.3 credits a line. Fine for one video; about 180 credits for a season. Take 4 uses Kokoro-82M running locally on the CPU — no account, no key, no quota, nothing to run out of, and re-cutting a line costs time instead of money.

It also sounds less rushed. The generated clips carry about a second of padding each; stripping that recovered 23 seconds across the episode, which bought a 0.65-second breath between lines instead of the 0.1 seconds the paid voice had been squeezed into.

Still to do

  • The ledger has a red smiley on it. In this video a red smiley is the killer's signature, so it implies Red John was behind the embezzlement. He wasn't. It sits on the subject, so it can't be cropped out.
  • The 27-second intro is unproven. It fixes comprehension; whether it costs more viewers than it keeps is a question only the retention graph answers.

The ending, and why it felt broken

Take 2 ended by repeating its own first line, to loop back to the start. Asked how that landed:

“It reads as the narrator losing his place. A sentence about the video, not a sentence in it. The video didn't end, it fell over.” On the old final line

So the loop is gone. Take 3 ends on the verdict as a challenge — “Eighty-twenty to Jane. Wrong? Say so.” — and then names the thread that isn't finished: “Red John is still out there. Episode two.” One is a provocation, the other is a full stop that points forward instead of backward.

What changed

Take 2 opened with“A man is shot dead in front of Patrick Jane.” A strong hook — attached to nothing. The viewer didn't know who Jane was, what show this was, or what the numbers meant.
Take 3 opens with“Rewatching The Mentalist. A fake psychic consults for the police. Jane, or his detectives? I'm scoring every episode. Episode one.” The show, the man, his job, the game and the format — in eight seconds.
  • Red John is now explained before he's used. Take 2 said “staged as Red John” and explained him a line later. He now gets introduced first, including the fact that he signs scenes with a red smiley — which is what turns “wrong wall” from a vibe into a deduction.
  • The scoreboard starts level. It used to open at 62/38 before a single event had happened, which is exactly why it read as a poll. It now starts 50/50 and carries a label: share of the solve.
  • The argument moved 20 seconds earlier. “The killer planted it” — the point of the whole video — used to arrive at second 63. It's now its own line at second 43.
  • The killer now gets caught on screen. Take 2 went from motive straight to the aftermath. The arrest was missing entirely.
  • The prison-cell image is gone from the video. It made the viewer believe Red John had been caught — the opposite of the truth, and the engine of the whole series.
  • No still is reused on back-to-back lines, and three reuses that read as “the video glitched” were eliminated by re-casting other frames.

Still wrong

  • Five images are still missing. The shooting, the wife with the gun, the planted hair, the wrong wall and the motive all still play on stills made for other beats. The prompts are written; the render swaps to them automatically once they exist.
  • The ransack has no image at all. “The killer is tearing his partner's office apart” plays over a room where nothing is disturbed.
  • One stray smiley survives at the verdict. The killer's signature appears on a background shelf in a frame where he was never present. The stills are 9:16 going into a square window, so the crop can only move vertically — this one needs a new image, not a new setting.
  • The intro costs eight seconds. That's the trade: the video now explains itself, but the shooting has moved from second 0 to second 8. Whether that's the right call is the thing to watch in the retention graph.

How the split is scored

Three weighted components, the same for every episode: identification (50%) — who worked out who did it; evidence and legwork (30%) — forensics, records, canvassing; resolution (20%) — who produced the confession or the arrest.

The forensic evidence was real, and it was aimed at innocent men — because the killer planted it. But the detectives confirmed the copycat staging from archive footage, an interrogation produced the affair, and one of them made the arrest Jane physically could not. That's the twenty, and it's arguable. That's the point.

The picture finally holds still

Take 2 calmed the cuts and it was still shaky, because the cuts were never the problem. useGateWeave re-randomised the whole frame’s x, y and rotation every two frames, for the entire video — a 12Hz jitter running continuously underneath everything, between the cuts rather than at them. It is a film-projector emulation, and it is not a Vox technique. Vox moves a still slowly in one direction and otherwise lets you look at the picture.

Gate weave is now off. The Ken Burns push stays, because a slow directional drift is the actual technique and without it a cut reads as a slideshow — but it is halved: a 4.5% push and 11px of travel across a shot, down from 9% and 22px. Measured between consecutive frames mid-shot, take 2 moved one to two pixels at a time and this moves zero.

The same argument at one seventh the length

Take 1 ran 8:10, which is not a Short — YouTube files anything over three minutes as an ordinary video. This is the identical case, identical verdict, cut to 26 lines and 355 words: exactly episode one’s density. Every beat of the spine survives. What went is the brother with the hatchet, the sheriff, the timecard, the locker note, and every agent introduction that was a clause of its own rather than a job done inside a line.

And the pictures have stopped moving

Three separate animations used to fire on every cut: the board jolted up to ten pixels in a random direction, the clipping scaled in from 1.08 on a spring curve that overshoots and springs back, and each one landed at a random lean of up to four degrees, animated in from a different angle. That is fine across episode one’s twenty-five cuts. Across take 1’s hundred and eight it never finished decaying before the next cut started, and it read as a camera nobody was holding still. The jolt is now zero, the scale-in is 1.02 on a curve that simply stops, and the lean is halved and static.
